Bumps and boundaries

A kernel lets the support vector machine draw curved boundaries while still solving the same problem as before. Choose a kernel, then turn C and γ and watch the boundary go from too stiff to too wiggly — and the test accuracy tell you which is which.

Feature space

(x⊤x′ + 1)² is an inner product between six features, (x1², x2², √2 x1x2, √2 x1, √2 x2, 1), so the boundary is a flat plane in six dimensions and a conic — an ellipse, parabola or hyperbola — here. Nothing six-dimensional is ever computed.

Feature space

The linear kernel is the ordinary inner product x⊤x′: no lift at all, and the boundary is a straight line whatever C is. This is the first demo, seen through the dual.

What the picture shows

The shading is the score z(x) the SVM gives every point of the plane: one colour where it predicts class +1, the other where it predicts −1, stronger the further z is from zero. The solid line is the boundary z(x) = 0 and the dashed lines are z(x) = ±1, the edges of the street from the first demo, now bent. Filled samples trained the model; hollow ones were held back to test it.

With a kernel the score is no longer w⊤x + b but a weighted sum over the support vectors,

z(x) = Σi ∈ S ai y(i) k(x(i), x) + b

so each support vector adds a little hill of its own sign. For the Gaussian kernel each hill is a bump of width about 1/√γ centred on the support vector. Show the bumps draws them. Free support vectors sit exactly on the dashed lines; bound ones, with ai = C, sit inside the street or on the wrong side.

What the curve is

For the Gaussian kernel the upper plot on the right sweeps γ with C held fixed, and plots the accuracy on the training samples and on the test samples. For the other kernels it sweeps C. The two curves agree while the model is too simple; as it becomes more flexible the training curve keeps climbing and the test curve turns down. The gap between them is overfitting, and the peak of the test curve is where you want to be. The grid below it does the same for C and γ together, which is what a grid search in the notes (Section 5.3) computes.

What the lift is

The Lift kernel is k(x, x′) = x⊤x′ + ‖x‖²‖x′‖², the inner product of the three features (x1, x2, ‖x‖²): the two-dimensional version of the map x ↦ (x, x²) in Figure 5 of the notes. On the circles, no line in the plane separates the classes, but lifting each sample to a height ‖x‖² raises the outer ring above the inner one, and a flat plane then cuts between them. The inset on the right shows that plane; where it meets the lifted surface is the circle you see as the boundary. The SVM finds the plane without ever forming the lifted points — it only ever evaluates k.

Things to try

  • On Moons with the Gaussian kernel, sweep γ slowly from left to right. Watch the boundary go from nearly straight to a set of islands around individual samples, and the test accuracy rise and then fall.
  • Try Too wiggly and compare training and test accuracy. Then press Draw a new sample a few times: the wiggly boundary changes completely from sample to sample, the sensible one hardly at all.
  • Turn on Show the bumps with a large γ. Each support vector has become its own island. Now lower γ and watch the bumps merge into a smooth surface.
  • Try Circles with the linear kernel: no line helps, and test accuracy stays near 50%. Switch to Lift and turn the inset: the classes now sit at different heights and a plane separates them.
  • On XOR, compare Lift with Polynomial. The lift has no x1x2 feature, so it cannot represent XOR; the polynomial kernel has one, and can.
  • Try Unscaled feature. With standardisation off, x2 is measured in tiny units, so it barely changes any distance and the Gaussian kernel ignores it: the boundary becomes vertical bands. Turn standardisation back on.
  • With a large C, count the support vectors, then lower C. More samples fall inside the street, more become bound support vectors, and the boundary smooths out — C and γ both control flexibility.

What this leaves out

Everything is two-dimensional, so the kernel's real advantage — working in a feature space far too large to form — is only hinted at. The test set here is a second draw from the same distribution; in practice C and γ are chosen by cross-validation on the training data, and the test set is touched once, at the end. The scores are not probabilities.

Samples are simulated, and training and test sets are drawn independently. Each fit solves the kernel SVM dual by sequential minimal optimisation in the page; the curve and grid refit the model once per point. Nothing is fetched or stored — refresh to reset.
