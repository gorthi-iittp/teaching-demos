What you are looking at

The diagram at the top is the network itself, drawn with its current weights: two inputs, one hidden layer of two neurons and one sigmoid output neuron. Each hidden neuron computes hᵢ = g(wᵢ·x + bᵢ), and the output is p̂ = σ(v₁h₁ + v₂h₂ + c). It is trained by batch gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss, with backpropagation supplying the gradient.

On the left, each dashed line is where one hidden neuron’s score crosses zero, so each hidden neuron on its own is a logistic-regression-style line. The network’s boundary, the solid curve, is not either of them: it is what the output neuron makes of the two together.

On the right, every sample is plotted at (h₁, h₂), the pair of numbers the output neuron actually sees. The output neuron is just logistic regression in this space, so its boundary here is always a straight line. The grey curves show what the hidden layer does to the whole plane: the square grid of the left panel is stretched, squeezed and bent until a straight line can separate the classes.

The take-home message

A hidden layer learns a new representation of the data in which the classes become linearly separable. The curved boundary in the input space is nothing more than a straight line in the hidden space, carried back through the hidden layer.

A limitation

Two hidden neurons can bend and squeeze the plane, but they cannot fold it. The map from (x₁, x₂) to (h₁, h₂) never sends two different regions on top of each other, so on Disc in a ring the inner class stays surrounded by the outer one in the hidden space too, and no straight line can cut it out. Training stalls with some points misclassified. A third hidden neuron lifts the data into three dimensions, where a plane can slice the disc off; that hidden space can no longer be drawn flat, which is why this demo stops at two.

Things to try

  • On XOR clusters, press One step a few times, then Run. Early on, all four clusters sit near the centre of the hidden space. Watch two diagonal clusters get pushed into one corner and the other two pulled apart until the output line can split them.
  • Point at a dot near the curved boundary on the left, then find it on the right. It sits close to the straight line there too: near the boundary in one space means near it in the other.
  • Press Train from scratch several times on XOR. Each press starts from new random weights. Some runs reach a clean fit by a different route; many end with one cluster stuck on the wrong side, however long you run. That is a local minimum: the loss of a network is not convex, unlike logistic regression’s, and where training ends depends on where it starts.
  • Switch the hidden activation to ReLU. The hidden space now has folds and creases rather than smooth bends, because ReLU zeroes everything on one side of each dashed line. Points on the wrong side of both lines collapse onto the single corner (0, 0).
  • Try Two moons, then Disc in a ring, and compare how far the grid can be bent in each case.
