What the street is

Any line that separates the classes has a closest point on each side. Slide the line to the middle of the gap and turn it, and the distance to those closest points changes. The SVM chooses the line for which that distance is as large as possible: the widest street that can be laid between the classes with no point inside it.

The points on the edges of the widest street are the support vectors. They alone decide the answer. Every other point can be dragged anywhere outside the street and the line will not move. In the canonical scaling of the notes the street edges are where y(x) = ±1, and its half-width is

half-width of the street = 1 / ‖w‖

so widening the street is the same as making ‖w‖ small. That is where the first term of the objective comes from.

What the curve is

The upper plot on the right shows, for every angle, the widest street you could get at that angle by sliding the line to the middle of the gap. A point on the curve is an angle, and the highest point is the SVM. Your line sits on the curve only when it is centred; Centre my line in the gap puts it there.

Below zero, no line at that angle separates the classes. The curve has corners rather than a smooth top because it is the smallest of several distances, one per point: each corner is an angle at which the closest point changes, and at the peak two or three points are exactly tied. Those tied points are the support vectors.

What C does

If the curve never rises above zero, no line separates the classes and the hard-margin SVM has no answer. The soft margin lets points into the street, or across the line, at a price. Each offender gets a slack ξ, drawn in red, and the SVM minimises

½‖w‖² + C Σ ξn

The bar shows the two terms. The first penalises a narrow street and the second penalises violations; C sets the exchange rate. A large C buys few violations with a narrow street, and a small C buys a wide street with many points inside it. Setting C to ∞ recovers the hard margin.

Things to try

  • Start with Clean gap, turn your line and slide it, and try to beat the curve's peak. Then snap to the SVM. You cannot beat it: the peak is the best any line can do.
  • Turn on Follow the SVM and drag a point far from the street, as far as you like. Nothing moves. Now drag a support vector, or push any point into the street, and the line moves at once.
  • Do the same with logistic regression shown. Its line shifts a little with every point you move, because its loss is never exactly zero: every sample has a say, where the SVM listens only to its support vectors.
  • Try One stray point. With C = ∞ the street shrinks to fit around a single point. Lower C and watch the SVM give up on that point in exchange for a much wider street.
  • With Overlapping classes, sweep C from small to large. Count the support vectors as you go: small C gives a wide street crowded with them, large C a narrow one with few.
  • Change the units of x2 and follow the SVM. The line turns, although the data only changed scale. The street is measured in Euclidean distance, so, as with PCA, features in different units should be standardised first.
  • Drop to four points per class and redraw a few times. With so few points the support vectors are most of the data, and the line swings from sample to sample.

What this leaves out

Everything here is a straight line in two dimensions. Curved boundaries come from replacing inner products with a kernel, which is the next demo. The scores y(x) behind the line are not probabilities, and nothing here involves more than two classes. The algebra, including the dual problem that the page solves, is in the lecture notes.

Points are simulated from two Gaussian clouds whose gap and scaling you control. The SVM is solved in the page by sequential minimal optimisation, and the hard-margin line directly from the geometry. Nothing is fetched or stored — refresh to reset.
